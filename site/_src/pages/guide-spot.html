1. Find the real website: it is at the end

Look at the name between https:// and the next /. The real website is the last two parts of that name (three for endings like .co.uk or .bank.in). In https://hdfcbank.com.secure-login.top/verify the website is secure-login.top, not hdfcbank.com. Everything in front of it is decoration anyone can add.

2. A brand name on someone else's website

paypal.com-secure-help.site and sbi-kyc-update.xyz both use a brand name, but neither is run by that brand. Banks and payment apps use a small set of their own domains; HDFC Bank, for example, uses hdfcbank.com and hdfc.bank.in. PhishGuard knows the real domains of {{brand_count}} brands.

3. Letters that only look right

paypa1.com uses the digit 1 for an l. amaz0n uses a zero. Some fakes swap in letters from other alphabets that look identical, such as a Cyrillic а in "аpple"; your browser may show these as xn-- addresses. Read slowly, letter by letter.

4. An unusual ending

Endings like .top, .xyz, .click, .icu and .zip are cheap and heavily used by scam sites. A bank will not send you to one.

5. An @ sign in the address

In https://bank.com@evil.example/login the browser ignores everything before the @ and opens evil.example. Real links almost never contain one.

6. Bait words

Words like verify, kyc, suspended, refund, reward and login in an address are designed to rush you. Banks do not ask you to "verify KYC" through a link in a message.

7. Shortened links and brand-new websites

A shortened link (bit.ly/…) hides where it goes. And many scam sites are only days old: a website registered last week asking for your bank password is a strong warning sign.

8. No padlock: http instead of https

A page asking for a password over http:// sends it unencrypted. Note the reverse is not true: most phishing sites today do have a padlock, so https alone proves nothing.

What an address cannot tell you

A perfectly normal-looking address can still lead to a fake login page, a malware download or a fake virus alert, because those only show up when the page loads. That is why the PhishGuard app opens every unknown link in a hidden browser first and shows you what is really there.

If you already typed a password into a page you now doubt, change that password straight away from the real website, turn on two-step sign-in, and tell your bank if card or account details were involved.
